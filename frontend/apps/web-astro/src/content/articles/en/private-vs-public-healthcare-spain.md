One of the first things new residents in Tenerife figure out is how healthcare works. In Spain it is strong, but two-tier: there is a public system and a private one. Let us look at how the island's public healthcare is organised, who gets it free, and when a private policy is really needed.

## Is healthcare free in Tenerife?

**For people who are entitled to it, yes — but entitlement is not automatic and does not come with the plane ticket.** Public care in Tenerife is free at the point of use for those covered by the Spanish system; everyone else is treated, but can be invoiced afterwards.

You normally have entitlement if you:

- **work or are self-employed in Spain** and pay contributions — plus your registered dependants;
- are a **pensioner from another EU country or the UK** and have registered an S1 form with the Spanish authorities;
- are a **child** living in Spain, regardless of your parents' status;
- pay for a **[Convenio Especial](/en/blog/convenio-especial-vs-private-insurance/)** — voluntary monthly contributions into the public system.

You normally do **not** have it if you are a tourist, a newly arrived non-resident, or a resident with neither contributions nor a Convenio Especial. Visitors from the EU or the UK with a valid EHIC or GHIC card are covered for medically necessary state care during the trip — not for planned treatment, and not as a substitute for [travel insurance](/en/blog/travel-insurance-coming-to-spain/).

Emergencies are a separate matter: an emergency department (*urgencias*) will treat you whatever your status. Being treated and being covered are not the same thing — without entitlement, the bill follows.

## The healthcare system in Tenerife: a regional service

**Healthcare in Tenerife is run by the Canary Islands, not from Madrid.** Spain has a national health system, but responsibility for it is devolved to the autonomous communities: each one runs its own hospitals, health centres, appointment systems and waiting lists. In the Canaries that is the regional health service, and its rules are what you actually deal with on the island.

For a newcomer this has three practical consequences:

- your **health card is regional**. It is issued in the Canary Islands and tied to the address where you are registered (*empadronamiento*);
- **moving regions means re-registering** — a card from Madrid or Valencia does not simply carry over;
- **emergency care works across Spain** regardless of which region issued your card.

Tenerife itself hosts two large public university hospitals — Hospital Universitario de Canarias in La Laguna and Hospital Universitario Nuestra Señora de Candelaria in Santa Cruz — so most specialist and hospital treatment stays on the island.

## Public and state medical centres in Tenerife

**The entry point to the public system is not a hospital but your local health centre — the *centro de salud*.** You are assigned to one by your registered address, and you cannot simply pick a different one because you prefer the doctors there.

How the route works in practice:

1. You register at the health centre for your address and receive an individual health card (*tarjeta sanitaria*).
2. You are assigned a **family doctor** (*médico de familia*) and a nurse. Everything non-urgent starts with them.
3. A **specialist, a scan or a scheduled procedure** comes by referral from that doctor, at the hospital or outpatient centre your area belongs to.
4. Outside health-centre opening hours, urgent problems go to hospital *urgencias*; the emergency number across Spain is **112**.

The system is solid, and for serious illness it is the layer that carries the weight. What it does not give you is choice — of centre, of doctor, or of appointment date. That is the difference people usually feel first, and the reason many pair it with a policy that offers a [network of private clinics](/en/blog/private-healthcare-tenerife/) instead.

## What is the public health charge in Tenerife?

**There is no single "public health charge" in Spain — the phrase usually means one of three different things.** It is worth knowing which one you are actually looking for, because the answers point in opposite directions.

1. **A visa health surcharge.** Britons often search for the Spanish equivalent of the UK's Immigration Health Surcharge. **Spain does not have one.** Instead, Spanish visas and residence permits require you to hold a private policy meeting set conditions — see [insurance for a residence permit](/en/blog/insurance-for-residency-visa/).
2. **A bill from a public centre.** If you are treated without entitlement, the regional health service can invoice you afterwards at its published public tariffs. The amount depends on the treatment and on the tariff in force, so there is no single figure to quote — and it is the scenario that catches out uninsured visitors most often.
3. **The Convenio Especial.** Voluntary monthly payments that buy a resident access to the public system when they have no automatic right to it. It has real limits — prescriptions at full price, the same waiting lists — set out in [Convenio Especial versus private insurance](/en/blog/convenio-especial-vs-private-insurance/).

Note the difference between the second and the third: one is a bill after the fact, the other a subscription arranged in advance.

## Public healthcare (Seguridad Social): strengths and limits

The Spanish public system is high quality, and basic care is free for those with access. Its strengths:

- free emergency and basic care;
- high standard of doctors and hospitals;
- cover for serious cases and hospitalisation.

The main drawback for many is the **waiting lists**: a specialist appointment or a scheduled test can sometimes take weeks. That is precisely the gap most people are trying to close when they start looking at a private policy.

## Private insurance

A private policy does not replace public healthcare but complements it — above all in speed and comfort:

- a specialist appointment within days;
- diagnostics and tests without long waits;
- doctors who speak your language;
- a good choice of clinics in Tenerife.

And to arrange many visas and residency a private policy is simply **mandatory** — public access does not replace it.

## When private insurance is really needed

- **You are arranging a visa or residency** — a private policy is almost always required.
- **You do not yet have Seguridad Social access** — for example, in the first months after moving, while the rest of the [relocation checklist](/en/blog/moving-to-tenerife-checklist/) is still in progress.
- **Speed matters** — you do not want to wait weeks for a specialist.
- **Language matters** — it is more comfortable to talk to a doctor without an interpreter.
- **You move between countries** — cover that follows you across borders is a separate product, [international health insurance](/en/blog/international-health-insurance-spain/).

> Many people combine both systems: public as a reliable base, private for fast access and planned health. It is a normal and widespread strategy.
